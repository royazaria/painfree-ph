Why Does It Hurt Only on the Right or Only on the Left?

The lower back is built from two almost identical sides: on each side there is a deep muscle, a sacroiliac joint, small joints between the vertebrae and a nerve root that runs down to the leg. When only one side hurts, one of these structures is usually more loaded or more irritated than its twin on the other side. The reason is most often habit: a bag on the same shoulder, a child you always carry on the same hip, sleeping on the same side, long drives with one foot on the pedal. These are the five most common causes:

1. A Locked Quadratus Lumborum Muscle

This muscle connects the lowest rib to the pelvic bone, deep beneath the large back muscles. When it contracts, the pain sits at the side, right above the rim of the pelvis, and gets worse when you get out of bed, turn over in your sleep or bend sideways. It is one of the most common reasons for “pain only on the left side” after lifting something heavy or a sharp twist. If the pain started all at once, read about acute low back pain and a back that locks up as well.

2. The Sacroiliac (SI) Joint

This is the joint that connects the sacrum to the pelvis — one on the right and one on the left. Here the pain is low, above the buttock, and sometimes travels into the back of the thigh, but almost never below the knee. It flares when climbing stairs, standing on one leg and getting up after sitting for a long time, and it is common in pregnancy, after childbirth and in people who walk unevenly. Read more: sacroiliac joint pain.

3. The Small Joints Between the Vertebrae (Facet Joints)

Every vertebra has two small joints at the back, one on each side. When one of them is worn or irritated, the pain is pinpoint — a few centimetres from the spine, on one side. It gets worse when you lean backwards, twist or stand for a long time, and eases when you bend forward or sit. It is more common after the age of 50. Read more: facet joint syndrome.

4. A Herniated Disc With Sciatica on One Side

A disc that bulges sideways presses on the nerve root on that side. The pain runs from the back into the buttock and from there down the leg, sometimes all the way to the foot, with numbness, tingling or a feeling of “electric currents”. Sitting, coughing and sneezing make it worse, and quite often the leg hurts more than the back itself. Read more: sciatica and a herniated disc.

5. Piriformis Syndrome

The piriformis is a small, deep muscle in the buttock, and the sciatic nerve runs underneath it. When it shortens and locks, it causes pain in one buttock that resembles sciatica — but the lower back stays almost quiet. Driving, sitting for long on a hard chair and crossing your legs make it worse. Read more: piriformis syndrome.

Right or Left — Does It Matter?

As far as the back itself goes, hardly at all: the same five structures exist on both sides, and what decides is which side you load. The important difference lies in front, in the abdomen. The kidneys sit on both sides, and on the right there are also the liver, the gallbladder and the appendix — which is why there are signs that the pain is not coming from the back at all (details in the red-flags section below). What Else Helps at Home on the Hard Days?

  • A warm compress on the painful side relaxes the locked muscle and makes getting up in the morning easier.
  • Gentle movement instead of bed: short walks through the day are better than lying down for long stretches, which stiffens the back.
  • Sleeping position: on the healthy side with a pillow between your knees, or on your back with a pillow under your knees.
  • Spread the load: a bag on both shoulders, switch the side you carry your child on, and get up from your chair every hour.
  • Painkillers and anti-inflammatories: only as your doctor or pharmacist advises, and for a short period.

🚩 Red Flags: When It Is Not Your Back, and When to See a Doctor Right Away

In most cases, pain on one side of the lower back comes from a muscle, a joint or a nerve. But there are situations where it comes from another organ, or signals serious pressure on the nerves — and then you do not wait:

  • Flank pain with fever, chills, burning when you pass urine, cloudy urine or blood in the urine — a possible kidney infection. See a doctor the same day.
  • Very sharp pain that comes in waves from the flank into the lower abdomen or the groin, with nausea or vomiting — a possible kidney stone.
  • Sudden, especially severe pain in the back and abdomen that is not related to movement — particularly over the age of 50 or in smokers — go to the emergency room.
  • Numbness in the groin, genitals or buttocks (the “saddle area”), loss of control over urine or bowel movements, or weakness in a leg that keeps getting worse — go to the emergency room immediately.
  • Pain that started after a fall or an injury, especially in someone with osteoporosis.
  • Pain with unexplained weight loss, night sweats, or in someone who has had cancer in the past.
  • On the right side: pain that starts near the navel and moves to the lower right abdomen with fever (possible appendicitis), or pain under the right ribs after a fatty meal (the gallbladder).
  • In women: pain on one side with unusual bleeding, or new pain during pregnancy — see a gynaecologist.

🔬 What Has Research Found on Pulsed Electromagnetic Fields (PEMF) in Low Back Pain?

The studies did not divide participants by the side of the pain. They examined non-specific low back pain — the group that most cases of muscle, joint or disc pain belong to. These are the three key studies:

  • A meta-analysis of 14 trials (Sun et al., Clinical Rehabilitation, 2022): 618 participants in randomised trials. Pulsed electromagnetic fields (PEMF) reduced low back pain more than a sham treatment, and also more than the other treatments they were compared with; in chronic back pain the reduction was statistically significant. The limit: no significant advantage was found in acute back pain, and no significant improvement was found in measures of physical function — the difference was measured in pain (see the study on PubMed).
  • A systematic review of 9 trials (Kull et al., Medical University of Vienna, Wiener Medizinische Wochenschrift, 2025): 420 participants with non-specific low back pain. In 5 of the 9 trials, PEMF significantly reduced pain and improved function compared with the control group, and no serious adverse event was reported in any of them. The limit: 4 trials found no advantage, the protocols differed widely from one another, and the review did not calculate a pooled estimate (see the study on PubMed).
  • A randomised trial in 50 patients (Elshiwi et al., Brazilian Journal of Physical Therapy, 2019): all participants, who had chronic low back pain, did physiotherapy; half also received PEMF and half a sham device. After 12 sessions over four weeks, the functional disability index in the PEMF group improved by about 8 points more, and lower-back range of motion also improved more. The limit: on the pain score itself the difference between the groups was not clear-cut, and the trial was run at a single centre (see the study on PubMed).

The trials were run on PEMF devices from different manufacturers and with different protocols.

Frequently Asked Questions About Lower Back Pain on One Side

Lower back pain on the left side — how do you know it is not your kidney?

Kidney pain is hardly affected by position: it stays whether you lie down or sit, and sometimes comes in waves that double you over. Muscle or joint pain, by contrast, flares with a particular movement and eases with rest. Fever, a change in the colour or smell of your urine, and nausea are reasons to see a doctor today.

And what if the pain is on the right side?

When it comes to muscles and joints, right and left behave the same way. What is special about the right side is the gallbladder and the appendix: pain that comes after fatty food, or pain in the lower right abdomen with fever, belongs with a doctor and not with a pain clinic. For more on this side, read about right lower back pain.

Why is the one-sided pain worse in the morning?

During the night the muscles and joints barely move, and the irritated tissue stiffens. That is why the first steps are hard, and after a few minutes of movement the back “opens up”. Stiffness like this is typical of a muscle or a joint. Pain that wakes you at night and does not change when you shift position is worth checking with a doctor.

Do the pulses replace physiotherapy?

No — the pulses don’t come instead of physiotherapy; they are what lets you do it and keep at it. In a randomised trial from Cairo University (Elshiwi et al., Brazilian Journal of Physical Therapy, 2019), pulses were added to a four-week physiotherapy programme, and the patients who received the active device finished with fewer limitations in daily life than those who received a switched-off device (see the study on PubMed).

How many sessions are needed, and what do you feel during treatment?

The treatment does not hurt and does not heat the body. The series of sessions is built for you personally at the suitability assessment, according to how strong the pain is and how long you have had it.

The pain only started yesterday. Should I start pulse treatment right away?

The pulses showed their result in pain that has lasted for months; in fresh pain of just a few days, the large meta-analysis found no significant advantage. In the first days, gentle movement, heat and partial rest come first, and if the pain has not gone within a few weeks — that is the time for a suitability assessment. The treatment does not enter the body and involves no medication, and in a review of nine trials in back pain no serious side effects were reported (see the study on PubMed).
