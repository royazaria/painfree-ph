Why Does the Lower Back Hurt When You Sit?

The body is built to move. When you stand and walk, the load on the spine is shared between muscles, joints and discs, and it shifts with every step. Long sitting does the opposite: the pelvis tilts back, the lower back loses its natural curve, and the pressure settles on the same tissues for hours. The more you slump — at a low screen, on a soft sofa, in the car — the more load lands on the back of the discs, while the muscles that are meant to steady the spine simply switch off. These are the most common sources of pain that builds up in the chair:

1. A Disc Under Pressure — Pain That Grows the Longer You Sit

Sitting loads the discs of the lower back more than standing does. A bulging or herniated disc answers with pain that builds through the sitting session and sometimes travels into the buttock and leg. The familiar sign: getting up is hard, the first few steps hurt, and after a few minutes of walking the back settles. Read more about a lumbar disc bulge.

2. Shortened Muscles and Muscles That Have “Switched Off”

During long hours in a chair the hip flexors at the front of the thigh shorten, while the glutes and abdominals barely work. When you stand up, the tight muscles pull the pelvis forward and the lower back takes the strain. This is the dull, stiff ache at the end of an office day that spreads across the back and eases with movement. Once it has lasted three months or more, it counts as chronic pain — see our chronic pain guide.

3. The Sacroiliac (SI) Joint — When You Sit on One Side

Crossing your legs, always leaning on the same armrest or keeping a wallet in your back pocket puts more load on one side of the pelvis. The result is low pain just above the buttock, on one side only, that flares as you go from sitting to standing. Read more about sacroiliac joint pain and lower back pain on one side.

4. The Tailbone — Pain Right Where You Sit

When the pain sits right at the bottom of the spine, between the buttocks, and gets worse as you lean back in the chair, the source is usually the tailbone rather than the lower back. It is common after a fall, after childbirth and in people who sit for hours on a hard surface. The full picture is in our guide to tailbone pain when sitting.

5. A Sciatic Nerve Irritated in the Buttock

A deep muscle in the buttock, the piriformis, runs right next to the sciatic nerve. Long hours on a hard seat, or driving with a wallet in your back pocket, can irritate the nerve. You feel it mainly in the buttock, with burning or tingling that runs down the back of the thigh — sometimes with hardly any back pain at all. Read more about piriformis syndrome.

How Should You Sit So Your Back Hurts Less?

The pulses bring the pain down; sitting well keeps it from coming straight back. A few small changes at your desk and in the car take load off the back from today:

  • Support the curve: a small cushion or a rolled-up towel at waist height keeps the natural curve of the lower back and stops it rounding backwards.
  • Chair height: feet flat on the floor, knees roughly level with the hips or slightly below them.
  • Screen at eye level: a low screen pulls the head forward and the whole back into a slump. On a laptop, use a stand and a separate keyboard.
  • Get up often: even a minute of standing, or a walk to the kitchen, takes the pressure off the discs and wakes the muscles up.
  • In the car: the seat close enough that your knees stay slightly bent, the backrest almost upright, and a short stop every hour on the road.
  • Don’t sit on a wallet or phone: anything in your back pocket tilts the pelvis to one side and presses on the buttock.
  • A standing desk: switching between sitting and standing through the day beats either one on its own.

🚩 When Does Back Pain From Sitting Need a Doctor?

Most pain that builds up in the chair is mechanical — from a muscle, a joint or a disc — and it changes when you change position. But there are signs where you do not wait and go to a doctor:

  • Numbness in the buttocks, groin or genitals (“saddle” numbness) or any change in control of your bladder or bowels — a possible sign of cauda equina syndrome. Go to the emergency room immediately.
  • Weakness in a leg, numbness that is spreading, or a foot that “drags” — especially when it gets worse from day to day.
  • Pain with fever, chills or unexplained weight loss, or a history of cancer.
  • Pain that started after a fall, an accident or an injury, especially with osteoporosis or long-term steroid use.
  • Night pain that has nothing to do with position — it does not change when you turn over and does not ease with rest.
  • New back pain that appears for the first time after the age of 50, with no clear reason.

🔬 Pulsed Electromagnetic Fields (PEMF) and Low Back Pain: What Did the Trials Find?

The research on pulsed electromagnetic fields (PEMF) looked at non-specific low back pain — pain that comes from the muscles, joints or discs, which is exactly the kind most people feel at the computer. These are the main findings:

  • A meta-analysis (Sun et al., Clinical Rehabilitation, 2022): 14 randomised trials, 618 participants. Pain fell more in the groups that received pulsed electromagnetic fields (PEMF) than in the sham groups, and the advantage was statistically significant in chronic pain. What it did not find: in acute back pain, and on measures of physical function, the difference did not reach significance (see the study on PubMed).
  • A systematic review (Kull et al., Wiener Medizinische Wochenschrift, 2025): 9 trials with 420 participants. In five of them, PEMF significantly reduced pain and improved function compared with the control group, and no serious adverse event was reported in any trial. What it did not find: four trials showed no advantage, the protocols varied widely, and the review did not calculate a pooled estimate (see the study on PubMed).
  • A randomised trial (Elshiwi et al., Brazilian Journal of Physical Therapy, 2019): 50 patients with chronic low back pain all received physiotherapy; half also received active PEMF and the other half a sham device. After four weeks, the disability score in the PEMF group fell by about 8 points more than in the sham group, and lower-back range of motion improved. What it did not find: on pain intensity itself the difference between the groups was not clear-cut, and the trial was run at a single centre (see the study on PubMed).

The trials used devices from different manufacturers, at different intensities and frequencies.

Frequently Asked Questions About Back Pain When Sitting

Why does my back hurt when I sit but feel better when I walk?

When you sit, the pressure on the discs of the lower back is higher than when you stand, and the muscles that hold the spine up barely work. Walking spreads the load and brings blood to the tissues, so the pain settles. That pattern usually fits mechanical pain from a disc or the muscles, not a systemic disease.

Will an ergonomic chair fix my back pain?

The pulses treat the pain that has already built up in the tissue; the chair only takes part of the load off it. A good chair with lumbar support certainly helps, but plenty of people buy an expensive chair and keep suffering. What changes the picture is a treatment that reaches the back itself — with the right chair and regular breaks on top.

Does PEMF replace physiotherapy?

No – the pulses don’t come instead of physiotherapy; they are what lets you do it and keep at it. In a randomised trial by Elshiwi et al. (Brazilian Journal of Physical Therapy, 2019), patients who received active pulses during a month of physiotherapy finished with less day-to-day disability than those treated with a switched-off device (see the study on PubMed).
