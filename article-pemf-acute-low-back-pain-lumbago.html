What Is Acute Back Pain (a Back Spasm) — and Why Does Your Back Lock Up Right Now?

“My back went out” — almost everyone has said this at least once in their life. A back spasm (medically: acute low back pain; popularly known as lumbago) is a sudden, sharp pain in the lower back that usually arrives in a single moment: bending to tie a shoelace, lifting a laundry basket, twisting the body quickly, a hard sneeze, or getting up after hours of sitting at the computer.

What actually happens is that the muscles running along the spine go into a protective contraction. The body “locks” the area to protect it, and the result is a stiff back, pain that worsens with every movement and a feeling that you cannot straighten up. Sometimes the source is a strained muscle or ligament, sometimes the small joints between the vertebrae, and sometimes simply a load that built up over weeks and “exploded” at the wrong moment.

The good news? In most cases a back spasm is not a sign of serious damage to the structure of the spine. It hurts a lot, it is frightening — but it is not your fate.

How to recognise a back spasm:

  • Sharp pain in the lower back, sometimes on one side only
  • Difficulty straightening up after bending
  • Walking “crooked” or the body leaning to one side
  • Pain that worsens when getting out of bed, sitting, coughing and sneezing
  • Hard muscles along the back that are tender to the touch

How Long Does Acute Back Pain Last — and When Is It No Longer “Just a Spasm”?

In most cases a back spasm eases gradually: the first days are the hardest, and over the following days and weeks the back starts moving again. The accepted recommendation today is to stay as active as the pain allows, and not to lie in bed for whole days.

But there is another side, and anyone who has lived it knows it well: for some people the pain never fully goes away, or it keeps coming back — with every lift, every long drive, every morning. Back pain that lasts more than three months is defined as chronic back pain, and at that stage it is already running your day: what you lift, how you sleep, whether you go out or stay home. What Else Can You Do at Home in the First Days?

The pulses do the work in the clinic — and at home, between sessions, a few simple habits help the back release:

  • Move gently: a short walk around the house or the building, several times a day. Movement keeps the muscles flexible and stops the back from “setting”.
  • Local heat: a heating pad or a hot shower calms the locked muscles for many people.
  • Painkillers — only as your doctor or pharmacist advises: follow the instructions and do not exceed them, in line with your own health situation.
  • A comfortable sleeping position: on your side with a pillow between your knees, or on your back with a pillow under your knees.
  • Back to the exercises: as soon as the pain allows, strengthening and posture exercises build a more resilient back and help prevent the next spasm.

When Does Back Pain Need a Doctor or the Emergency Room Right Away?

Most back spasms are not dangerous, but some signs require an immediate medical examination:

  • Weakness in a leg, a leg that “drags”, or difficulty lifting the foot
  • Numbness or loss of sensation in the groin, buttocks or genital area
  • A new problem controlling urine or bowel movements
  • Fever, chills or feeling generally unwell together with the back pain
  • Severe night pain that does not change with movement or a change of position
  • Pain that started after a fall, an accident or a blow
  • A history of cancer, or unexplained weight loss
  • Pain that radiates into both legs

In any of these situations — go to a doctor or the emergency room immediately. Pain that gets worse day by day, or shows no improvement at all after a few weeks, is also a reason to be checked by your family doctor or an orthopaedist. If the pain travels down from the back into the leg, read about the symptoms of a herniated disc as well.
