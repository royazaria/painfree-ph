Why Does the Back Hurt Specifically at Night?

During the day your back moves all the time: you stand up, walk, bend. Movement pumps blood into the muscles and nourishes the joints. At night everything stops for six or seven hours. Tissue that is already irritated stiffens, the discs absorb fluid and swell slightly, and a poor position loads the same spot for hours on end. The quiet plays its part too: with no noise or work to distract you, the brain notices every bit of pain. These are the most common causes of back pain at night:

1. Tired Muscles and Stiff Joints

This is the most common cause. After a day of sitting at a computer, driving or physical work, the lower back muscles are overloaded, and when you lie down they tighten. The pain is dull, spread across the back, gets worse when you turn over and eases after a few minutes of movement in the morning. If the pain sits on one side only, read about lower back pain on one side as well.

2. A Herniated Disc and Sciatica

A bulging disc presses on a nerve root, and the pressure changes with your position. For some people, lying on the back with straight legs makes the pain worse; for others, it is lying on the side. The telltale sign is pain that runs down into the buttock and the leg, with numbness or tingling — sometimes the leg wakes you more than the back does. Read more: sciatica and a herniated disc.

3. Wear in the Vertebrae and the Small Joints (Facet Joints)

After the age of 50, wear in the small joints between the vertebrae is very common. Lying on your stomach arches the back and presses exactly on them, and turning over hurts too. Anyone with this kind of wear knows the mornings: you get up stiff, and it takes a quarter of an hour for the back to “open up”. Read more: facet joint syndrome.

4. The Sacroiliac (SI) Joint

Low pain above the buttock, on one side, that flares every time you turn over in bed or lie on the painful side. It is common in pregnancy, after childbirth and in people who walk unevenly because of another pain. Read more: sacroiliac joint pain.

5. Inflammatory Pain That Wakes You in the Small Hours

There is one kind of back pain that behaves the opposite way to all the others: it wakes you in the second half of the night, comes with long morning stiffness, and gets better with movement rather than with rest. When it starts before the age of 45 and lasts more than three months, it can be inflammatory back pain, as in ankylosing spondylitis — and then it needs to be checked by a rheumatologist, because pain like this has its own dedicated medication. Read more: ankylosing spondylitis.

Until the Pulses Bring the Pain Down — What Helps Tonight?

The pulses bring the pain itself down; the right pillow only takes load off the back. So while you treat the source, these are the habits that will help you from the very first night:

  • On your back: a pillow under your knees relaxes the lower back and takes pressure off the vertebrae.
  • On your side: a pillow between your knees keeps the pelvis level and stops the back from twisting.
  • Not on your stomach: this position arches the back all night long. If it is hard to give up, a thin pillow under your stomach and pelvis reduces the arch.
  • Roll to get up: turn onto your side, lower your legs off the bed and push up with your arms to sit — instead of hauling yourself up with your back.
  • Warmth before sleep: a hot shower or a heating pad for a quarter of an hour relaxes the muscles before you get into bed.
  • A medium-firm mattress: not sagging, and not as hard as a plank.
  • Painkillers: only as your doctor or pharmacist advises, and for a short period.

🚩 Red Flags: Night Pain That Needs a Doctor

Most back pain at night comes from muscles, joints or a disc. But night pain is also one of the signs doctors take seriously, so it is worth knowing the cases where you do not wait:

  • Pain that is constant, does not change in any position, does not ease with rest, and gets worse from night to night.
  • Pain with unexplained weight loss, night sweats or fever, or in someone who has had cancer in the past.
  • New back pain that starts after the age of 50 with no clear reason.
  • Pain that started after a fall or an injury, especially with osteoporosis or long-term steroid use.
  • Numbness in the groin, genitals or buttocks, loss of control over urine or bowel movements, or weakness in a leg that keeps getting worse — go to the emergency room immediately.
  • Pain with burning when you pass urine, blood in the urine or abdominal pain — the source may be the kidney or another organ, not the back.
  • Morning stiffness that lasts more than half an hour in younger people, with pain that improves with movement — to be checked by a rheumatologist.

🔬 What Has Research Found on Pulsed Electromagnetic Fields (PEMF) in Low Back Pain?

No trial looked at “back pain at night” as a separate group. The studies examined non-specific low back pain — the group that most cases of muscle, joint or disc pain belong to, at night and during the day. These are the three key studies:

  • A meta-analysis of 14 trials (Sun et al., Clinical Rehabilitation, 2022): 618 participants in randomised trials. Pulsed electromagnetic fields (PEMF) reduced low back pain more than a sham treatment, and also more than the other treatments they were compared with; in chronic pain the reduction was statistically significant. The limit: no significant advantage was found in acute pain, and no significant improvement was found in measures of physical function — the difference was measured in pain (see the study on PubMed).
  • A systematic review of 9 trials (Kull et al., Wiener Medizinische Wochenschrift, 2025): 420 participants with non-specific low back pain. In 5 of the 9 trials, PEMF significantly reduced pain and improved function compared with the control group, and no serious adverse event was reported in any of them. The limit: four trials found no advantage, the protocols differed widely, and the review did not calculate a pooled estimate (see the study on PubMed).
  • A randomised trial in 50 patients (Elshiwi et al., Brazilian Journal of Physical Therapy, 2019): all participants, who had chronic low back pain, did physiotherapy; half also received PEMF and half a sham device. After 12 sessions over four weeks, the functional disability index in the PEMF group improved by about 8 points more, and so did lower-back range of motion. The limit: on the pain score itself the difference between the groups was not clear-cut, and the trial was run at a single centre (see the study on PubMed).

The trials were run on PEMF devices from different manufacturers and with different protocols.

Frequently Asked Questions About Back Pain at Night

Why does my back hurt only when I lie down?

When you lie down, the back stops moving and the muscles that worked all day tighten. An irritated disc swells slightly at rest, and a worn joint is locked at the same angle for hours. That is why pain you barely felt during the day wakes up in bed. If the pain changes when you shift position, it is almost always mechanical — from a muscle, a joint or a disc.

What is the best sleeping position with back pain?

The least load on the back comes from lying on your side with your knees slightly bent, or on your back with your knees raised. Lying on your stomach is best given up. But a position only eases the load — and if the pain wakes you even in the right position, it is time to treat the pain itself with pulsed electromagnetic fields.

The pain wakes me before dawn and gets better when I get up and move. What does that mean?

Pain that wakes you in the small hours, with long morning stiffness and improvement with movement, is typical of inflammatory back pain — especially when it started before the age of 45. Pain like this is checked by a rheumatologist, because inflammatory pain has medications aimed precisely at it, and those are not stopped. And if the assessment shows the pain is mechanical — from a muscle, a joint or a disc — that is exactly the back pain in which pulsed electromagnetic fields were tested.

I bought a new mattress and the pain did not go away. Why?

Because in most cases the mattress did not create the problem; it only made it worse. A good mattress takes load off, but a tight muscle, a worn joint or a disc pressing on a nerve keep hurting even on the most expensive mattress. The pain is in your back, and that is where the treatment needs to reach — which is exactly what pulsed electromagnetic fields do.

Does PEMF replace physiotherapy?
