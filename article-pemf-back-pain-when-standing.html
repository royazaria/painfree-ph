Why Does the Lower Back Hurt After Standing for a Long Time?

It sounds strange — standing is supposed to be the “healthier” position compared with sitting. But the back dislikes any position held for hours without moving. When you stand in one spot, the same muscles hold the spine up non-stop, and the weight of the upper body rests on the same points the whole time. Many people also stand with the belly pushed out and the pelvis tipped forward, which deepens the arch of the lower back and shifts the pressure onto the back of the vertebrae. A hard floor, flat shoes and end-of-day fatigue only add to it. These are the usual sources of pain that shows up after an hour or two on your feet:

1. Muscles That Work Without a Break

When you walk, the muscles tighten and relax in turn, and blood flows into them with every step. When you stand still, they stay contracted for a long time, tire and start to ache. This is the dull pain that spreads across the waist, grows as the shift gets longer and eases once you sit or lie down. When it comes back every day for months, it has become chronic back pain.

2. The Facet Joints — When the Arch in Your Back Deepens

Behind each vertebra sits a pair of small joints, the facet joints. Standing with an exaggerated arch, and especially leaning backwards, presses them against each other. The pain is focused in the lower back, sometimes on one side, gets worse when you stand or bend backwards, and improves when you lean slightly forward. The full picture is in our guide to facet joint syndrome.

3. Spinal Stenosis — When Your Legs Feel Heavy as You Stand

In people over 50, the canal that the nerves run through can narrow over the years. Standing upright and walking narrow it even further, bringing pain, heaviness, numbness or weakness in the buttocks and legs. The tell-tale sign: you bend forward, lean on the shopping trolley or sit down — and within minutes the legs settle. Read more about lumbar spinal stenosis.

4. The Sacroiliac (SI) Joint — When You Stand on One Leg

If you tend to stand with all your weight on one leg, or carry a child on your hip, one side of the pelvis takes the load. The pain sits low, just above the buttock, and sometimes spreads into the thigh. Everything you need is in our guide to sacroiliac joint pain, and if the pain is always on the same side, see also lower back pain on one side.

5. Worn Discs and a Back That Struggles With Load

Over the years the discs dry out and lose height, and the back copes less well with prolonged load. In some people this shows up precisely as pain that builds through a day on their feet. Read more about degenerative disc disease, and if the pain travels down the leg, about sciatica and a herniated disc.

How Should You Stand So Your Back Hurts Less?

The pulse treatment brings the pain down, and a few simple habits at work help keep it from returning quickly. You can start with them on your next shift:

  • A small footrest: put one foot on a step or a low box and switch feet every few minutes — the arch of the lower back flattens and the facet joints get some relief.
  • Soft knees: standing with the knees locked back pushes the pelvis forward. A slight bend takes part of the load off the back.
  • Move on the spot: a few steps, rising onto your toes or a small pelvic circle every ten to fifteen minutes wakes the muscles up and gets blood flowing into them.
  • Floor and shoes: an anti-fatigue mat on a hard floor, and shoes with a cushioned sole and arch support.
  • A work surface at the right height: a counter or worktop that is too low forces you to bend forward for hours. Where you can, raise whatever you are working on.
  • Sit when you get the chance: a bar stool or a lean-to perch next to your station lets the back rest for a few minutes without leaving your post.
  • Strengthen your core: strong abdominal and gluteal muscles keep the pelvis level and take load off the back all day long.

🚩 When Does Back Pain From Standing Need a Doctor?

Pain that appears after hours on your feet and eases with rest is usually mechanical — from the muscles, the joints or the discs. Even so, these are the signs that send you to a doctor without waiting:

  • Numbness in the buttocks, groin or genitals, or sudden difficulty controlling your bladder or bowels — a suspected cauda equina syndrome. Go to the emergency room immediately.
  • Weakness in a leg, a foot that drags, or numbness that keeps spreading — especially when it is getting worse from day to day.
  • Leg pain that quickly shortens how far you can walk and forces you to stop and sit after only a few minutes.
  • Pain with fever, chills or unexplained weight loss, or a background of cancer.
  • Pain that began after a fall, an accident or a blow, especially with osteoporosis or long-term steroid use.
  • Night pain that does not depend on position and does not ease even when you lie down.
  • New back pain after the age of 50 that appeared with no clear cause.

🔬 Pulsed Electromagnetic Fields (PEMF) and Low Back Pain: What Did the Trials Find?

Pulsed electromagnetic fields (PEMF) were tested in randomised trials against a sham treatment in non-specific low back pain — pain that comes from the muscles, joints or discs rather than from a systemic disease, which is also the usual kind of pain felt when standing. The trials did not focus specifically on people who stand for hours at work. These are the main findings, each with its limits:

  • A meta-analysis (Sun et al., Clinical Rehabilitation, 2022): the researchers combined 14 randomised trials with 618 participants and found that pain fell more in the PEMF groups than in the sham groups. The advantage was significant in chronic pain. What it did not find: in acute back pain the difference was not significant, nor was it on measures of physical function; and within the chronic-pain group, results varied widely from trial to trial (the study on PubMed).
  • A systematic review (Kull et al., Wiener Medizinische Wochenschrift, 2025): the review covered 9 randomised trials with 420 participants. In five of the nine, PEMF significantly reduced pain and improved function compared with the control group, and none of them reported a serious adverse event. What it did not find: four trials showed no advantage, the protocols differed widely, and the review did not calculate a combined result across the trials (the study on PubMed).
  • A randomised trial (Elshiwi et al., Brazilian Journal of Physical Therapy, 2019): 50 patients with chronic low back pain went through four weeks of physiotherapy; half of them were also treated with active PEMF and the other half with a switched-off device. At the end of the period the disability score in the PEMF group had improved by about 8 points more than in the sham group, and lower-back range of motion increased as well. What it did not find: there was no clear difference between the groups in pain intensity itself, and the trial was run at a single centre (the study on PubMed).

The trials used devices from different manufacturers, with different intensities, frequencies and numbers of sessions.

Frequently Asked Questions About Back Pain When Standing

Why does my back hurt when I stand but settle when I sit down?

During long standing, the muscles that stabilise the spine work without a pause, and the arch of the lower back loads the joints at the back of the vertebrae. Sitting flattens the arch a little and lets the muscles rest, so the relief comes quickly. When the legs feel heavy and only settle when you sit or bend forward, narrowing of the spinal canal is worth considering too.

Will insoles or good shoes solve the problem?

The pulses treat the pain that is already in the back, while an insole or a good shoe only reduces the shock coming up from the floor. Cushioned shoes and an anti-fatigue mat certainly help, especially on concrete or tiles. But people whose back has hurt for months usually find that shoes alone are not enough, and that they need a treatment that reaches the back itself.

Do the pulses come instead of physiotherapy?

No — the pulses don’t come instead of physiotherapy; they are what lets you do it and keep at it. In the trial by Elshiwi et al.
