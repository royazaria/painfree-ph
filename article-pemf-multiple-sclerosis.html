Why MS Patients Seek Non-Pharmacological Adjuncts

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune demyelinating disease affecting approximately 2.8 million people worldwide. In the Philippines, prevalence is estimated at 4,000–7,000 diagnosed cases — significantly underdiagnosed relative to the population of 115 million due to limited neurology access outside Metro Manila and Cebu. The two symptoms that dominate clinical quality-of-life scores are fatigue (reported by 75–80% of patients) and spasticity (35–85%), neither of which responds adequately to disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) alone.

Disease-modifying drugs — interferons, natalizumab, ocrelizumab — slow relapse rates but do not reliably address fatigue or spasticity. Amantadine and modafinil for fatigue show modest effects with tolerability issues. Baclofen and tizanidine for spasticity carry sedation and weakness risks. This therapeutic gap creates significant demand for evidence-based, non-pharmacological adjuncts — which is precisely where PEMF sits.

Mechanism: How PEMF Affects MS Symptom Pathways

MS symptoms arise from three interacting processes: axonal demyelination (disrupting nerve conduction velocity), neuroinflammation (perpetuating lesion expansion), and mitochondrial dysfunction (underpinning fatigue at the cellular level). PEMF addresses all three through distinct biophysical pathways:

  1. Mitochondrial ATP upregulation — PEMF fields in the 10–50 Hz range increase cytochrome c oxidase activity, raising mitochondrial membrane potential and restoring ATP synthesis in axons already stressed by demyelination. This is the proposed mechanism for fatigue reduction.
  2. Neuroinflammation modulation — PEMF suppresses microglial M1 activation and reduces secretion of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the CNS, attenuating perilesional inflammation without systemic immunosuppression.
  3. Calcium signaling normalization — Demyelinated axons exhibit dysregulated Ca²⁺ influx contributing to spasticity; PEMF normalizes voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel activity, reducing hyperexcitability in spinal motor circuits.
  4. BDNF and NGF upregulation — PEMF increases brain-derived neurotrophic factor and nerve growth factor expression, supporting myelin repair processes and axonal survival in perilesional zones.

Clinical Evidence: Fatigue and Spasticity

The most cited double-blind RCT in MS-PEMF literature (Lappin et al., Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine, 2003) enrolled 37 patients with relapsing-remitting MS, randomized to 25 Hz PEMF (20 minutes/day, 8 weeks) or sham. The primary outcome — Modified Fatigue Impact Scale (MFIS) — showed statistically significant reduction in the active group versus sham (p=0.04). Bladder and bowel function scores also improved significantly in the PEMF arm.

A second double-blind RCT (Piatkowski et al., 2009, n=27 secondary-progressive MS) applied low-frequency PEMF over 4 weeks. Spasticity measured by the Modified Ashworth Scale decreased significantly in the PEMF group (mean 2.5 → 1.9, p=0.016); the sham group showed no change. Patient-reported pain and sleep quality also improved in the active group.

A 2014 systematic review of electromagnetic therapy in MS (Richards et al., Multiple Sclerosis Journal) concluded that the evidence base for PEMF in MS fatigue is "promising," noting methodological limitations in available RCTs but consistent directional results across studies. The overall evidence classification is Level B (moderate evidence, adjunct role).

Clinical Protocol for MS Patients

Phase 1 — Anti-inflammatory and Fatigue Foundation (Sessions 1–8, Weeks 1–4)

  • Frequency: 10–25 Hz
  • Intensity: Low to moderate (1–5 mT)
  • Duration: 20–30 minutes per session
  • Coil placement: Full-spine mat (cervical + thoracic + lumbar) for systemic neurological effect
  • Session frequency: 3× per week (minimum), 5× per week for faster fatigue response
  • Goal: Establish mitochondrial baseline; reduce neuroinflammatory burden

Phase 2 — Symptom-Targeted Protocol (Sessions 9–18, Weeks 5–9)

  • Frequency: 25–50 Hz (spasticity focus) or 10–25 Hz (fatigue focus)
  • Intensity: Moderate (3–8 mT)
  • Duration: 30–40 minutes
  • Coil placement: Spinal (spasticity) + localized limb coil for affected extremities
  • Session frequency: 2–3× per week
  • Goal: Target dominant symptom cluster (fatigue vs. spasticity vs. pain)

Phase 3 — Maintenance (Ongoing, Monthly)

  • Frequency: 25 Hz maintenance
  • Duration: 20–30 minutes
  • Session frequency: 1–2× per week or fortnightly boosters
  • Goal: Sustain symptomatic gains; schedule intensification during relapse periods

Important Positioning: PEMF as Adjunct, Not Disease Modifier

It is critical that PEMF is positioned accurately with MS patients and their neurologists. PEMF does not slow relapse rates, halt lesion accumulation, or replace disease-modifying therapy. The evidence supports PEMF as a non-pharmacological symptom management adjunct — addressing fatigue, spasticity, and pain — while patients remain on their prescribed DMT regimen.

This positioning is commercially and ethically important. Clinics should coordinate with the patient's neurologist and document symptom tracking (MFIS for fatigue, Modified Ashworth Scale for spasticity) to demonstrate measurable outcomes and sustain the referral relationship.

Contraindications and Precautions in MS

  • Active relapse: Defer PEMF until 4–6 weeks post-relapse stabilization; acute inflammatory lesions may respond unpredictably
  • Active pacemaker or implanted neurostimulator (DBS/VNS): Absolute contraindication
  • Intrathecal baclofen pump: Consult device specifications; many pump manufacturers advise avoiding strong electromagnetic fields near the device
  • Severe heat sensitivity (Uhthoff phenomenon): Ensure PEMF system does not generate significant thermal effect; most clinical PEMF systems operate at sub-thermal levels
  • Pregnancy: Contraindicated per standard PEMF safety guidelines

Philippines Market Context

MS in the Philippines remains severely underdiagnosed: the Philippine Neurological Association estimates 4,000–7,000 identified cases, but true prevalence may be 2–3× higher due to limited access to MRI and neurological specialists outside NCR and key urban centers. As diagnostic capacity improves with Health Facilities Enhancement Programme investments, the diagnosed MS population is expected to grow materially through 2030.

MS patients in the Philippines carry a disproportionate symptom burden relative to global counterparts: later diagnosis (mean delay of 3–5 years), limited access to high-efficacy DMTs, and reliance on generic interferons with suboptimal adherence due to cost. This makes non-pharmacological symptom management (PEMF, physiotherapy, occupational therapy) even more clinically and commercially significant in the Philippine context than in Western markets.

Rehabilitation centers affiliated with neurology departments at Philippine General Hospital, St. Luke's, The Medical City, and Chong Hua Hospital Cebu represent the primary clinical partnership channel for PEMF clinics targeting the MS segment. With 70+ Israeli clinics (population: 9M) now expanding to the Philippines, this protocol brings validated adjunct MS care to a growing and underserved neurological patient base.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can MS patients feel PEMF during the session?

Most MS patients do not feel PEMF during treatment — there is no heat, vibration, or electrical sensation at therapeutic intensities. Patients with heightened sensory sensitivity (a common MS symptom) occasionally report mild tingling, which resolves immediately upon session end and is not clinically significant.

How quickly does fatigue improvement appear?

The Lappin 2003 RCT used an 8-week protocol with significant fatigue reduction. In clinical practice, patients often notice subjective improvement in fatigue within 3–4 weeks of 3× weekly sessions. Objective MFIS score improvement is typically measurable at 6–8 weeks. Maintenance sessions sustain the effect; discontinuation typically leads to gradual return of fatigue over 4–8 weeks.

Is PEMF safe alongside ocrelizumab or natalizumab?

No known electromagnetic interaction with any current MS disease-modifying therapy has been documented. PEMF does not affect drug pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics. However, clinics should document current medications and coordinate with the treating neurologist, particularly for patients on investigational agents or enrolled in clinical trials.
